# About Text Chart tab

*Using Tools › Texts & Words tools › Interlinear Texts › Text Chart tab*

In addition to the [Text Chart tab overview](Text_chart_overview.md) topic, this Help topic is included to provide an overview of the purpose and basic structure of **Text Chart** tab. The **Text Chart tab overview** topic lists the typical steps you need to do, with links to the more detailed topics.

## Purpose - General

Discourse analysis has as its focus linguistic phenomenon at greater than sentence level. This means that the structure of entire texts, conversations, and stories is of interest. Some of the questions that are part of discourse study are:

- How does the text hold together?

- How are "less important" parts of the story presented in relation to the "backbone" of the story?

- How are the various participants (characters) of a text referred to in the text?

- How does this language show which things are in focus?

- How are the different parts of the story linked together?

- How does the story reflect the cognitive knowledge structures of the speaker/author?

One of the primary tools that a linguist uses to answer these questions is the discourse chart.

There are almost as many different kinds of charts as there are linguists, but one of the primary types of chart is the Text Chart. The Text Chart is made by putting the text in a table so that (generally) each clause is on a separate line with the various phrases in the clause spread out to their own columns. This allows the linguist to see at a glance if certain verb tenses or aspects entail "different" behavior elsewhere in the clause, or if certain particles always show up at a certain point in a story, but not in others, etc. It also makes it easy to see if something (e.g. a Noun Phrase) occurs out of its "normal" position.

## About the Text Chart tab

### Structure

- The top pane contains the [columns and rows](Text_Chart_tab_columns_and_rows.md) which comprise the chart.

- The bottom pane contains the vernacular interlinearized words from the text selected in the center **Texts** pane. The vernacular words appear in the order they appear in the **Baseline** tab.

  - The word glosses appear (*currently limited to the default (top) analysis writing system*, and are *not* changed by the **Configure Interlinear Lines** dialog box). **See:** [Change Text Chart gloss writing system](Change_Text_Chart_gloss_writing_system.md). **See Also:** [Configure Interlinear Lines](../../../../User_Interface/Menus/Tools/Configure_interlinear_lines_dialog_box.md).

- Various [colors](Text_Chart_tab_colors_lines.md) are used for the data in the chart.

- Three different [line weights](Text_Chart_tab_colors_lines.md) are used between the rows.

- The column that has no header label indicates the sentence and clause. For example, **1c** is the first sentence, third clause.

- **See Also:** [Add a new Text Constituent Chart template](../../../Lists_tools/Text_Chart/Add_a_new_Text_Constituent_Chart_Template.md).

### Features

- The footer (bottom) of each column is a button. When you click one of them, you insert all selected vernacular words and their associated word glosses from the bottom pane into the corresponding column. In the bottom pane, the first word and its gloss are automatically selected, but you can select multiple sequential words and insert them into the chart at the same time. As you insert words and glosses into the chart, they are removed from the bottom pane, and then next word is selected automatically. The vernacular words and their glosses are presented in the chart in the order that they occur in the spoken text. Each new clause, even one without a verb, begins on a new line.

- Right-click menus enable you to accomplish many of the tasks listed as topics below.

### Marking in the chart

- Some implicit items, such as implicit subjects and verbs, are automatically marked by three dashes: - - - . You can also manually [mark as missing](mark_as_missing.md) other implicit items

- You can also mark cell content to indicate tense, aspect or mood, or as pronouns or demonstratives.
